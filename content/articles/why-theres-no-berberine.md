Berberine is the ingredient everyone expects in a metabolic formula right now, and it has real
research behind it. We still left it out. We would rather explain that here than let you find
the gap on the label and assume we were cutting cost.

## One · the format fights the dose

The doses in the literature are typically 500 mg taken two or three times a day, and
gastrointestinal complaints are the effect most commonly reported at that level. Crave Balance
is one drink, once a day, with lunch. A dose large enough to matter would make the format
unpleasant. A dose small enough to be comfortable would be theatre — a name on a label doing
marketing work rather than physiological work.

## Two · it needs a pharmacist

Berberine inhibits CYP3A4, the enzyme pathway a long list of prescriptions depends on. Our
reader may be on hormonal contraception, thyroid medication, or metformin. An ingredient that
genuinely requires a clinician's read before the first serving does not belong in an
unsupervised daily powder bought online.

> If an ingredient only makes the label longer, it is not an ingredient. It is copywriting.

## Three · it isn't the job

Crave Balance is built around inositol at the 40:1 ratio the trials actually use, with theanine
and saffron for the part of a craving that isn't hunger. Berberine would be a second, unrelated
mechanism bolted on so the front of the pack could say more words. That is precisely how
proprietary blends happen — and once you have a blend, you stop being able to publish a dose
card.

If we change our minds, you will read it here first, as a dated change-log entry — not as a
quiet reformulation between production runs.
